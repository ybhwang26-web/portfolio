Bolted Joints: Tension and Shear

How preload, joint stiffness and the applied load decide what a bolt actually carries. Worked through on my subscale TCA joint (converging nozzle bolted to the chamber with 12 tapped ¼-28 bolts).

Built from my handwritten notes (Bolt_Joint.pdf), my calculation sheet (rough_calc_2.xlsx, which follows the MechaniCalc bolted joint reference), and the Vespula injector PDR, slides 24–27, as a second example.

01

Preload: why tightening a nut makes a clamp

Look at it from the bolt's point of view. The head is held by the upper plate, so when the nut is rotated it pulls the bolt down. The bolt stretches, and like a rubber band it wants to go back to its original length. So the head pulls the upper plate down and the nut pushes the lower plate up. The plates are squeezed together, and that squeeze is the clamping force.

Preload is the tensile force this puts in the fastener. The same thing happens with a bolt in a tapped (threaded) hole: the threads in the hole play the role of the nut.

The plates are stiffer than the bolt, so for the same force Fi the bolt stretches a lot and the plates compress only slightly. In the spring model on the right, the two deflections are drawn in the ratio of my joint's stiffnesses: δp/δb = kb/kgrip ≈ 0.18.

02

Tension joint: who carries the applied load?

In a tension joint the applied load P pulls along the bolt axis. Without preload, P just pulls the plates apart. With preload, the bolt and the clamped plates act like two springs, and P is shared between them:

Fb = bolt load, Fc = clamping force, Fi = preload, kb = bolt stiffness, kp = plate stiffness, Δ = extra stretch

P = 0:   Fb = Fc = Fi
P > 0, Fc > 0:   Fb = P + Fc,   Fb = Fi + kbΔ,   Fc = Fi − kpΔ  ⇒  Δ = P/(kb+kp)
C = kb/(kb+kp):   Fb = Fi + C·P,   Fc = Fi − (1−C)·PC = joint stiffness factor
Fc = 0 at Psep = Fi/(1−C);   after that Fc = 0, Fb = Pthe bolt carries everything

The slope of the blue line is C. In my joint C ≈ 0.15, so the bolt picks up only about 15% of P and the clamp loses the other 85%. This is the point from the video (3:00–3:29) in my notes: the plate spring gets most of the load. As P grows, the plates head toward separation and the clamp drops.

The factor of safety against separation is FSsep = Fsep/Ft,app. In the PDR example the same method gives FS ≈ 1.29 at hard start. My joint has far more margin (about 13.5) because the design load is small compared with the preload.

03

Joint stiffness: where C comes from

The bolt is two springs in series: the unthreaded shank and the threaded part that sits inside the grip. Shank: A = πd²/4 over Lshank = L − Lthd. Threads: At over Lthd,g = Lg − Lshank.

kb = 1 / (1/kshank + 1/kthd),   kshank = (πd²/4)·Eb/Lshank,   kthd = At·Eb/Lthd,g
kgrip = π·E·d·tanα / 2 ln[ 5 (Lgtanα + 0.5d) / (Lgtanα + 2.5d) ],   α = 30°every part in the grip has the same E
C = kbolt / (kbolt + kgrip)

My tapped joint (redrawn from notes p.5). In a tapped joint the grip Lg is not the full bolt length: it is the head-to-plate length plus part of the engagement. My sheet uses Lg = 1.125 in.

The plates are about 5.6× stiffer than the bolt, which is why C is small.

04

Applied load on each bolt

Two loads act on my nozzle joint (notes p.6). Chamber pressure pushes on the sealed area and tries to pull the nozzle off, which loads the bolts in tension. The weight of the converging and graphite nozzle hangs off the joint, which gives a bending moment M = W·Lcom and a small shear.

FP = AP·pc,   AP = π dseal² / 4,   pc,max = 260 psi
Moment on a bolt circle: F = M·y / Σy²,   Σy² = 6R² for 12 bolts  ⇒  top bolt gets M/(6R)
Ft,app = FP/12 + M/(6R),   Fs,app = W/12

Hover or focus a bolt to see its load. Bolts above the center line get extra tension from M, and bolts below get less.

Pressure dominates. The moment adds less than 2 lbf to the worst bolt, and the weight shear is about 1 lbf per bolt.

05

Shear joint: friction first, then the bolt

In a shear joint the load P is perpendicular to the bolt axis. Preload matters here too, because the clamp creates friction between the plates.

Fi = preload, P = total load / number of bolts, μ = friction coefficient between plates, ns = number of slip planes

Slip-resistant:   Ff = μ·Fi·ns,   P ≤ μ·Fi·ns
Slipping:   P = μ·Fi·ns
Bearing joint:   the plates have slid until the hole walls touch the bolt, so P goes into the bolt
Slip-resistant

The slider is a ratio, so the picture holds for any μ. My notes don't give a μ value for my plates, so I don't compute a friction capacity here. In my sheet the shear is checked directly on the bolt, conservatively: τsh = Fs,app/Am ≈ 35 psi.

Design rule from my notes: keep threads out of the shear plane. If the shear plane cuts the threads, the area left is the minor area Am, which is smaller than the shank.

06

Preload and stress in the bolt

Choosing the preload

Target the preload that puts a tensile stress of 70% of yield in the bolt. My notes give three reasons for not going higher. In service the bolt sees torsion on top of the axial load. A torque wrench has about ±25% error. And thread lubrication and friction change how much of the torque becomes preload.

FPL = 0.70·Sy·At,   At = (π/4)(d − 0.9743/TPI)²
T = KT·d·FPLKT: nonplated 0.30, zinc-plated 0.20, lubricated 0.18, anti-seize 0.12

Why a nut factor: preload is an axial force, but the torque you apply is tangential at the bolt head. KT lumps thread geometry and the friction in the threads and under the collar into one number. I use 0.18 (lubricated), the same as the PDR, where all bolts are lubricated with Krytox.

Combining the stresses

σPL = FPL/At,   σt = C·Ft,app/At,   τsh = Fs,app/Am,   σbnd = 32Mb/πd³
σVM = √( [σPL + n(σt + σbnd)]² + 3(n·τsh)² )n = load factor that makes σVM = Sy

Preload alone uses 70% of yield, and the service load adds less than 1%. The factor n says the applied loads (tension and shear together) would have to grow about 32× before the bolt yields. σbnd = 0 because there is no gap between the plates (Mb = Fb,s·a/2 with a = 0).

The ±25% torque error

If the wrench under-delivers, the preload can be as low as 0.75·FPL, and the separation margin drops with it. The PDR notes the trade-off: lower torque raises the bolt-yield and thread-shear FOS but lowers the separation FOS, and higher torque does the opposite.

07

Thread shear: the bolt should break before the threads strip

Design goal: the bolt fails in tension before its threads shear. The threads in a tapped hole share the load over the engagement length LE, which is hnut for a nut or min(tp, d) for a tapped joint. My sheet uses LE = 0.75 in.

External (bolt):   Ats,ext = (5/8)π·dp·LE,   τ = Fb,t/Ats,ext,   FS = Ssy,bolt/τ
Internal (tapped hole):   Ats,int = (3/4)π·dp·LE,   τ = Fb,t/Ats,int,   FS = Ssy,grip/τ
Fb,t = FPL + C·Ft,apptotal tension in the bolt

Check: which fails first?

My own check, using the sheet's areas and strengths: the force that yields the bolt (Sy·At) compared with the force that strips each thread (Ssy·Ats). The bolt yields first, so the goal is met. The tapped threads in the 4130 chamber are the weaker of the two thread sides (FS 2.84), because the annealed 4130 is much weaker in shear than the A574 bolt.
